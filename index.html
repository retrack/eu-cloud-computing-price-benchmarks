How to read a run

Published storage prices are not comparable as printed. Three things differ between vendors and quietly move the figure by 10 % or more:

  • The unit. Several vendors bill in binary units while printing “GB”; others state no basis at all. Decimal versus binary is a 7.4 % difference on the same physical bytes. Rows whose vendor never states a basis carry a △ marker and follow the page's unit selector.
  • The month. Hourly-billed vendors are extrapolated using 720, 730 or 744 hours depending on who does the extrapolating. This benchmark uses 730 throughout, and the selector lets you change it.
  • What the headline excludes. For object storage: egress, requests, retrieval fees, minimum storage durations and minimum billable object sizes. For block storage: provisioned IOPS, provisioned throughput and snapshots, which at the top of the range cost several times the capacity itself. Both are shown alongside the storage rate.

Anything a vendor does not publish is shown as not published, never estimated, and a scenario total containing one is prefixed ≥ rather than silently treated as zero.

The two series answer different questions and their numbers do not compare. Object storage bills the bytes you actually hold; block storage bills the size of the volume, empty or full. A block volume also carries a performance ceiling, so a cheap row is not a substitute for an expensive one unless it can serve the workload — the block pages mark the rows that cannot.

Maintained by Antoine Coetsier, who works at Exoscale; Exoscale's rows are highlighted in the tables. Figures are taken from competitors' own published pages and every source is listed at the bottom of each run. Treat this as a starting point for your own verification, not as an authority — list prices ignore negotiated discounts and commitment terms, which are where large deals are actually decided.

Readings go stale silently: a run more than a month old should not be relied on.
